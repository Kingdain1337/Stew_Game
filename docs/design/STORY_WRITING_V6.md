# Story writing brief: a meal draws company

This is the current design direction, not a claim that a story is fun without playtesting. The plot should be simple enough to remember after a run and flexible enough to make each boss and ingredient choice feel like part of the journey.

## The Long Way to Town

A traveler loses the road, cooks from forest forage, and earns a ride with a merchant by cooking for her caravan. The smell draws bandits; feeding the whole group turns them into escorts. At the next camp, a blue forest troll arrives hungry enough to threaten the travelers. Large bowls satisfy it, and it follows the caravan. The company grows, its supper becomes a feast, and a great dragon lands nearby. The dragon recognizes that the old pot is unusual. It demands large servings and controls the fire twice; if fed, it flies the traveler to town.

The story's spine is one repeated action with a changing consequence: cooking draws someone new. Each arrival increases the size of the company and the risk of the next meal. The dragon is the climax, not the answer to a hidden mechanical mystery. The ending pays off the original goal—reaching town—and the friendships made on the way.

| Arc | Encounters | Story change | Game expression |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A place on the wagon | Forest meal, merchant, bandits | Alone → companion → escorted caravan | Foraged opening stock/offers; bandit supper needs three scored ingredient types |
| The company grows | Caravan camp, troll | More mouths and a dangerous new follower | Troll requires large servings |
| A dragon at supper | Growing feast, dragon | The road becomes a celebration, then a trial | Dragon requires large bowls and locks heat on bowls two and four |

Seven encounters are intentional. The story determines the number of chapters and boss positions; the royal story retains its ten-chapter structure. The road has its own target curve, tuned against one-deck runs in both pots rather than inheriting a ten-chapter curve.

## Tone and presentation

- Treat the premise as real within the world. The joke is that a cooking smell keeps recruiting increasingly unlikely traveling companions; characters do not explain the joke.
- Make the traveler and merchant's friendship concrete through shared work and a seat on the wagon. The bandits' escort and the troll's following are consequences of being fed, not sudden changes in their nature.
- The troll and dragon can understand speech and make choices but remain unmistakably nonhuman in body and behavior. The troll is tall, blue and beastlike; the dragon is a large quadruped with wings, scales, claws and its own fire.
- Keep inanimate objects unpersonified unless a story specifically makes them magical. The pot is special because of what it does, not because it talks.
- Chapter cards carry the plot. Speech bubbles show what a character notices during scoring and should never have to explain an entire scene.

## Deck fit

The playable setup now chooses one themed deck alongside the shared Pantry. Each story names one story deck and two recommended alternatives: road uses Char Hearth, with Culture Cellar and Bellglass Chorus as alternatives; royal uses Culture Cellar, with Char Hearth and Bellglass Chorus as alternatives. These are design recommendations using the existing deck packages, not newly invented or story-exclusive card sets. Deck compatibility is tested per story rather than assumed universal. A dedicated new deck should be added only when its cards and mechanics express a story beat better than these packages.

## Playtest questions

Can players retell the journey in a sentence? Does the change from bandits to escort feel earned? Are the bandit variety, troll portion, and dragon heat demands easy to predict from their chapter cards? Do small bowls remain tempting before a large-bowl boss? Does the dragon's arrival feel like a natural escalation rather than another arbitrary customer? Watch players before adding more scenes or systems.
